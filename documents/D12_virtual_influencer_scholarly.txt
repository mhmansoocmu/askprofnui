WHAT ARE VIRTUAL INFLUENCERS?

Non-human, digitally created characters that share social media content and engage in interactive communications with the aim of obtaining influential status among consumers.

Also called: digital humans, CGI influencers, AI influencers, synthetic media personalities.

---
KEY SCHOLARLY DEFINITIONS (cite these)

Mouritzen et al. (2024): "Non-human digitally created characters sharing social media content and engaging in interactive communications" with influential status among consumers.

Byun & Ahn (2023): "Digitized entities with anthropomorphized appearances that have human bodies, social roles, and their own identity."

Audrezet et al. (2025): "Digital personas created through computer-generated imagery and/or AI."

1996: Kyoko Date — first virtual idol (Japan, Horipro).
2003: Lu do Magalu — Brazilian retail VI; world's biggest VI by 2022.
2016: Lil Miquela — Brud (LA); AI/media focus.
2018: Laila Blue — Middle East's first AI influencer.
2023: Miss Lala — Dubai, LaLaVerse.
2025: Danah — Kuwait's first AI influencer (Abdulaziz Al-Rashed).

Regional relevance: GCC and Middle East adoption contexts for CMU-Q students.

WHY TAM ALONE MAY NOT BE ENOUGH

TAM asks: perceived usefulness and ease of use. But is a virtual influencer a "technology" in the traditional sense?

Students should justify framework choice. Often combine:
- UTAUT (social influence, facilitating conditions)
- HMSAM (hedonic motivation)
- CASA (social responses to digital agents)
- Parasocial relationship theory (entertainment/media studies)

---
CASA THEORY AND VIRTUAL INFLUENCERS (Nass et al., 1994)

People treat computers and mediated agents as social actors — applying politeness, gender stereotypes, and social norms.

Implication: Users form parasocial bonds with VIs; social influence constructs from UTAUT become central. Explains why authenticity, relatability, and visual human-likeness matter even when audience knows the influencer is not human.

RESEARCH FINDINGS (PEER-REVIEWED)

Lee & Yuan (2023):
- Female virtual influencers display more positive affect and disclose virtual nature more often.
- Receive more sexualized comments; more negative comments when displaying sexual cues.

Sabie & Iancu (2025):
- Female VIs portrayed with emphasis on beauty/sexualization.
- Male VIs linked to authority and emotional detachment.

Miyake (2023):
- Virtual influencers can reflect digital-orientalist commodification of race and gender.

Use these to support critical analysis — not just descriptive adoption factors.

FIVE FACTORS FOR ASSIGNMENT 2 (examples to develop with YOUR framework)

Students must identify exactly FIVE factors from their assigned IT adoption framework:

1. Social influence — peers and community normalize following VIs; UTAUT subjective norm.
2. Hedonic motivation — entertainment, escapism, parasocial enjoyment (HMSAM).
3. Perceived usefulness — product recommendations, information value (TAM/UTAUT performance expectancy).
4. Anthropomorphism / social presence — human-like cues trigger CASA social responses.
5. Facilitating conditions — platform algorithms (TikTok, Instagram), device access, payment for virtual gifts.
6. (Alternative) Trust/authenticity paradox — audience knows VI is fake yet engages socially.
7. (Alternative) Cultural fit — GCC vs Western VI aesthetics and modesty norms.

Pick five that map clearly to your framework's constructs — do not list generic factors without framework linkage.
